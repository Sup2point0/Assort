# *Lux* – All the Reasons Why I Love Light Mode

> [!Note]
> I’ve been meaning to write this for many years, but never got round to it. I don’t know if I ever will, and indeed many of my original thoughts will now be lost to me. Welp, what can you do. Here is the disjointed stuff I did put together.

It never occurred to me that “dark mode” was a thing. Light mode was just completely natural to me.

Now, just to clarify, I have no intention of denouncing dark mode here – I’m only expressing my opinions on why I like light mode more. Dark mode is awesome, and there are certainly situations where I like it (coding in particular)! In fact, to understand why I love light mode, we have to look at what dark mode does well, and how and why light mode does it differently; so there’ll be quite a bit of comparison here.


Let’s tackle the first point everyone always raises: *my eyes*.

In other words, light mode is way too bright for their eyes, to the point that it’s blinding.


The crucial difference lies in the way light and dark backgrounds contrast with colour. Here’s a hot take: when there’s no colour, I see a white background and a black background slightly differently – aside from the evident disparity in ‘colour’ and brightness, while black feels *empty*, white feels *blank*. There’s a subtle difference between the two, I feel. The emptiness of black indicates void, like there’s nothing there at all, yet the blankness of white suggests it’s waiting to be filled, like a blank canvas. Of course, neither of these is ‘better’ than the other, they’re just different vibes.

{ light and dark fills contrast }

I should make sure to clarify what I mean by contrast – it’s when 2 elements (mostly colours) are visually distinct from each other, which helps to identify each as its own element. So what this all means is that, once colour is added, the effect is quite different. With a light background, it feels like it’s settled in the white, like paint on a piece of paper. For a dark background, any bit of colour contrasts tremendously. It’s down to the fact that white is always *lighter* than other colours, while black is always *darker* – and so any particular colour will appear comparatively brighter when placed against a dark background than a light background.

{ supcode colours contrast }

This is why dark mode really makes all those colours *pop*, and those full-saturation block colours have such an *impact*, an effect you can’t achieve *as much* in light mode (that is to say, you still can, just not as much). That’s why code editors look so much better in dark mode – I’d even go so far as to say that coding in light mode is an abomination! (Shocking, I know.) But syntax highlighting is one of those circumstances where dark mode fulfils one of its purposes *so* well: providing high contrast to distinctly separate elements. In light mode, those same colours aren’t distinctive enough against the bright background, so to compensate, they’re darkened. But this results in another problem, where although the colours now have higher contrast with the light backing, they lose contrast between themselves. With syntax highlighting, what you want *is* that highly distinct contrast.

{ syntax highlighting contrast }

Another way in which this is highlighted (ha, yep, pun intended) is through that text highlighting! Changing the colour of dark text on white can be really quite jarring. Again, it’s since any colours appear really bright against black, and any text that is not black will contrast less with white, in a way undoing the intended emphasis. On the flip side for white text on a dark backing, changing the text colour is the perfect way to emphasise it. It flows so well while still preserving contrast, both between the light text and dark backing, and the white and coloured text.

{ text highlighting }

So, it seems like I’ve been praising dark mode a whole lot. And that’s entirely correct, since bringing out contrast is exactly what dark mode is wicked at. Contrast is cool and all, but light mode can do some pretty awesome stuff too.

Just above, I mentioned text highlighting as an aside. That happens to highlight (this is the last time, I promise) a key difference in the way colours can be utilised in light and dark mode – the way we highlight text in light mode is by... literally using a highlighter, or underlaying it with a block of colour.

See, what light mode is really great for, is harnessing colours as an accent. In light mode we can take a decent chunk of a page, make it one of our vivid full-saturation colours, and it wouldn’t look too bad with a bit of careful formatting. In dark mode, that’s kinda be difficult to fix without just darkening the colour, and even then it’d look a bit out of place.

{ accents contrast }

I think it’s because colours end up contrasting *too much* in dark mode, so a chunk that large is just too much. But darkening them mutes their vibrancy and kills the life of the design. Light mode inherently doesn’t have this issue to the same severity, so can get away much more easily with large blocks of colour.

This naturally reduced contrast also gives light mode a ‘calmer’ and ‘cleaner’ feel. In particular, I’ve seen some wonderful designs that create a lovely smooth and mellow atmosphere, utilising soft pastel-like colours; I can’t help but feel like that just wouldn’t be possible in dark mode.

{ Scratch contrast }
[ This is Scratch 3.0, which made one of the best colour overhauls to a UI I’ve seen. Ever. The new colours are super smooth and feel right at home alongside the calm blue-tinted greys, even the more saturated ones like the operator green and variable orange. Of course, colour isn’t the only thing at play here – the rounded edges and open spacing certainly play a role – but it really shows how the lightness of light mode facilitates a clean and pleasant overall look. In dark mode, everything just contrasts too much, and it’s as if they’re all fighting for attention. ]

TLDR: Dark mode is really good for text and fine lines, light mode is really good for blocks of colour.


Oh, and don’t even get me started on the greys. Actually, I think grey works equally well in both light and dark mode.
